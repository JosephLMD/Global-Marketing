Burger wars:
the fight one side is not having

For fifty years Burger King has been picking a fight with McDonald's. McDonald's has twice the restaurants, five times the ad budget and ten times the revenue, and mostly does not answer. This week you are not building a campaign about it. You are learning to measure it, which is the part everybody skips.

01 · Start here

Same shape as Nike and Adidas. One thing is different.

You already did half of this once. With Nike and Adidas you benchmarked, found the gap and then built a campaign, all in one delivery. This time they are split on purpose. This week is the benchmark and nothing else. Everything that comes after will be built on what you hand in now, so it is worth doing properly.

The other difference is that one benchmark is no longer enough. The analysis has to be segmented, because a 19-year-old and a 42-year-old do not want the same thing from a burger, and Bogotá is not Chicago.

What you have to do

Build six benchmarks, not one

Three age segments (18 to 24, 25 to 34, 35 to 44), each one looked at globally and in Colombia. Three times two is six.

Give every segment three good attributes and three bad ones

For each brand, in each segment. That is what turns a table into an argument: you are forced to say out loud where your own brand loses.

Say where the real opportunity sits, and for whom

Not the biggest segment. The one where the distance between the two brands is smallest, or where a strength turns out to be worth less than it looks. Name the segment, name the brand it favours, and defend the choice.

Not this week: the campaign. No name, no brief, no promotional pieces. If you hand one in it will not add marks, and it usually means the benchmark got less attention than it needed. Get very good at this part first. It is the part that decides whether everything after it stands up.

Words you are going to need

Benchmark
Comparing two brands against the same list of attributes. If you score one brand on price and the other on flavour, you have not benchmarked anything.
Attribute
One specific thing a customer can judge: speed, price, taste, cleanliness, how the app works, whether the ice cream machine is broken.
Challenger brand
The one that is not winning and therefore gets to break the rules. Burger King has been one for fifty years.
Brand intimacy
MBLM's measure of the emotional bond a brand has. McDonald's scores high on nostalgia. Burger King scores below average on almost everything.
Hackvertising
Burger King's own word: inserting the brand into something that already exists instead of buying attention. Google Home, competitors' ads, McDonald's own car parks.
Share of voice
How much of the category's total advertising is yours. McDonald's spends five times what Burger King does, so it starts every year already louder.
02 · The story

Seventy years, in the order they happened

Read this once before you touch the benchmark. Half of what looks like brand personality today is actually the residue of who owned the company that decade.

1940
Two brothers open a restaurant in San Bernardino
Richard and Maurice McDonald. In 1948 they drop most of the menu, focus on hamburgers and launch the Speedee Service System, applying production-line thinking to food. That is the moment fast food is invented.
1953
Someone visits that restaurant and copies it
Keith Kramer and Matthew Burns open Insta-Burger King in Jacksonville, Florida, inspired by what they saw in San Bernardino. Burger King begins as an imitation of McDonald's, and the position never really changes.
1955 · 1962
Ray Kroc arrives and the Golden Arches go up
Kroc joins as a franchisee in 1955, buys the brothers out in 1961, and introduces the Golden Arches in 1962. McDonald's dates its own founding to Kroc, not to the brothers.
1959 · 1967
Burger King starts changing hands
Miami franchisees James McLamore and David Edgerton buy the faltering company in 1959 and shorten the name. Eight years later they sell to Pillsbury. It is the first of many owners, and that instability becomes part of the story.
1965 · 1967
A clown, and a machine to pay for him
Ronald McDonald makes his national debut at the 1965 Macy's parade and the 1966 Super Bowl. In 1967 franchisees begin paying 1% of earnings into a national advertising fund: $5 million grows to $15 million by 1969 and $60 million by 1974. This is the five-times ad budget being built, brick by brick.
1974
"Have it your way"
Answering the Big Mac jingle's rigid recipe with a jingle about flexibility: "Hold the pickles, hold the lettuce. Special orders don't upset us." Burger King defines itself as the opposite of whatever McDonald's just said.
1982
The first attack ad in the category
Burger King names McDonald's directly and claims its burgers are 20% smaller. A four-year-old Sarah Michelle Gellar is in the spot. McDonald's sues for what it calls false and disparaging advertising. The suit settles, and same-store sales go up.
1985
"Where's Herb?" and a 40% profit collapse
A $40 million campaign built around a fictional man who had never eaten a Whopper. Six weeks of teasers, a Super Bowl reveal, a nationwide hunt. It said nothing about the food and nothing about the customer. Profits fell 40% in 1986. Learn this one: it is the clearest example of a big idea with no strategy under it.
2002 · 2010
Sold, and sold again
Investment firms buy Burger King for $1.5 billion in 2002; 3G Capital buys it in 2010. Each owner brings a new agency and a new idea of what the brand is.
2003
McDonald's first quarterly loss, and "I'm lovin' it"
After the loss, a global agency competition is won by Heye & Partner, a German subsidiary of DDB. The line has anchored McDonald's marketing for nearly twenty years. Consistency is the strategy.
2004 · 2009
Crispin Porter + Bogusky, and the internet
Subservient Chicken, the Whopper Sacrifice Facebook app, the plastic-faced King, a fictional band, video games for Xbox. Almost a decade of work that predicted how brands would behave once social media took over.
2012
Burger King loses second place
Wendy's passes it by US sales volume. The rivalry Burger King keeps picking is with the brand two places above it.
2018
Whopper Detour
Geofencing offers a 1-cent Whopper, but only if you are standing inside a McDonald's. It drove 1.5 million app downloads and made customers physically go to the competitor in order to reject it.
2020
Moldy Whopper, then the pandemic
A photograph of their own burger rotting, to prove the preservatives are gone. It won at Cannes and the Clios. Weeks later covid arrives and Burger King sales drop 25% in the second quarter.
2020
McDonald's recovers faster, on plumbing not creative
Drive-thru, delivery and digital, what the company calls the three Ds, were already built. Meanwhile Wieden+Kennedy launches Famous Orders with Travis Scott, and it becomes a platform.
2021 · 2022
Another reset
Global CMO Fernando Machado leaves in April 2021. After a review, the Chicago agency OKRP is named lead in April 2022, with the job of making Burger King culturally relevant again.
03 · How each brand thinks

One speaks like a leader. The other cannot afford to.

"McDonald's was the leader for the longest time, so they spoke with a voice of someone who is a leader in the market. They don't engage in the rivalry... Burger King, on the other hand, being the underdog, got to have all the fun."

Fernanda Bürgel, creative director, PeterMayer
McDonald's
Does not answer, and does not need to
  • Consistency as a weapon. "I'm lovin' it" has run for nearly twenty years. Rather than replace it, they keep finding new ways to interpret it.
  • Nostalgia. MBLM finds this is where McDonald's is strong, and it is one of the hardest bonds for a competitor to attack.
  • Famous Orders. The premise is that everyone already has a McDonald's order, famous or not. It borrows celebrity without renting a personality.
  • Infrastructure over ideas. Drive-thru, delivery and digital were built before they were needed, and that is why the pandemic hurt less.
  • Stable agency relationships. Long partnerships, chosen deliberately.
Burger King
Has to be interesting to be noticed
  • Defined by contrast. "Have it your way" exists because the Big Mac recipe was rigid. The identity is built against something else.
  • Hackvertising. Getting into culture through the side door instead of buying the front door, because the front door costs five times more.
  • Willing to be disliked. A photo of mould on your own product is not a normal decision.
  • No stable owner. Pillsbury, TPG, 3G. Each one restarts the brand.
  • The real problem. MBLM finds Burger King below average on most archetypes, failing to stand out for any particular attribute. Famous is not the same as differentiated.

The sentence the whole case turns on

"Burger King is commenting on culture, but culture is commenting on McDonald's." . Paul Prato, PPK. Read it twice. It says the awards and the applause have not moved the thing that matters.

04 · Where they fight

Five battlefields, and only one is still open

Every ratio comes from the article. Note what this means for your benchmark: Burger King cannot win any attribute that money buys. It can only win attributes that money cannot buy.
1

Money and scale

Twice the restaurants, five times the advertising, ten times the revenue. This one is not a fight, it is a fact.

McDonald's, permanently
2

Attack advertising

Burger King started it in 1982 and never stopped. The whole category now does it: Wendy's on Twitter, Jack in the Box on broken ice cream machines, Arby's hiring a rapper to write a diss track.

Burger King invented it, everyone copied it
3

Telling the truth about the food

Moldy Whopper and Keep It Real Meals against Famous Orders. One brand argues about ingredients, the other argues about belonging.

Split, and worth studying
4

Digital and delivery

Whopper Detour was brilliant and drove 1.5 million downloads. The three Ds were boring and saved the year. Both matter.

McDonald's, on the boring half
5

Collaborations

Borrowing somebody else's audience. Celebrity meals, film and cartoon licences, fashion drops, toy tie-ins, influencers. McDonald's built a whole platform on it with Famous Orders; Burger King answered with Keep It Real Meals. It is the cheapest way for a brand to appear somewhere it was not invited.

Both, constantly, and worth counting
05 · The benchmark

Six benchmarks. Here is exactly what that means.

One benchmark tells you which brand is stronger. Six tell you where and with whom, and that is the difference between a report and an opportunity. Nobody launches a campaign at "everyone".

This is the whole of Question 1, drawn. If a grid is empty, that part of the argument does not exist.

One grid, started for you

This is 18 to 24, global. There is one entry filled in each box so you can see the shape of a good answer. The rest is yours. Notice what the filled ones have in common: an attribute, and then the evidence for it in one line. Never just the attribute.

That is one of the six. Now do it five more times: 25 to 34 and 35 to 44 globally, and all three again for Colombia. The four entries above are yours to keep if you can defend them, but a delivery where those four are the only strong lines is a delivery that read the case and stopped there.

The rule that makes it a benchmark. That top row is not decoration. Both columns have to be judged on the same attributes: score McDonald's on "app quality" and Burger King on "sense of humour" and you have written two descriptions, not a comparison. Which attributes land as strengths and which land as weaknesses will differ between the brands, and that difference is the finding. The list they come from has to be the same.

What changes when you cross the border

The global grid you can build from reading. The Colombian grid you cannot, and that is deliberate. Some questions to answer with your own research, not with assumptions:

  • How many restaurants does each brand actually have in Colombia, and in which cities? The global ratio almost certainly does not hold here.
  • Who operates each brand locally, and what does that change about pricing, menu and speed of decision?
  • Which local competitors matter here that do not exist in the article? A Colombian benchmark that ignores the local burger chains is missing the real fight.
  • How do people actually order, in store, drive-thru, or through a delivery app? This changes which attributes even matter.
  • Is price perception the same across the three age groups? A 19-year-old student and a 42-year-old with children are not buying the same thing.
  • Put the two menus side by side, the global one and the Colombian one. Which products exist there and not here? Which exist only here? A menu is a series of decisions, not an accident, and the items that are missing were left out by somebody on purpose.
  • Which collaborations has each brand actually run here? Go and find them: local fashion labels, Colombian celebrities and creators, the film releases they tied themselves to. A global list of collaborations tells you nothing about whether any of it ever reached Bogotá, and the ones that did are the ones your segments actually saw.

Go and look. Walk into both. Open both apps. Ask ten people in each age group. The teams that do this always end up with a better campaign than the teams that guess, and it is obvious from the first slide which is which.

How to read the six grids once they are full

Look for the smallest gap, not the biggest market

A huge segment where one brand is far ahead on every attribute is not an opportunity, it is a wall. The interesting segment is the one where the two columns look close.

Look for a weakness only one of them has

If both brands are weak on the same thing, that is a category problem, not an opportunity. If only one is weak, that is an opening, and it works in whichever direction the grid points.

Check whether global and Colombia disagree

When they do, that is the most interesting finding in your whole analysis. Say so out loud instead of averaging it away.

Pay attention to what you cannot score

If an attribute is impossible to judge in Colombia because the thing simply is not sold here, do not leave the cell empty and move on. Write down what is missing. An absence is not a hole in your analysis, it is a result, and it is usually the result nobody else in the room noticed.

06 · What comes next

The tanker nobody has managed to turn

"Brand intimacy is more of a long term measure; think of it more like trying to turn a tanker. That's good news when you're managing crises... but it's also bad news if you're trying to turn this thing in the right direction."

Mario Natarelli, managing partner, MBLM

Three pressures on both brands

A

Sustainability

Neither brand has planted a flag. Bürgel compares it to the first man on the moon: whoever solves it first owns it. And she notes both are still on their first idea.

Wide open
B

Plant-based food

McPlant and its equivalents. A real product question and a real positioning question at the same time.

Contested
C

Effectiveness against excellence

Prato's warning: Burger King went looking for an agency to deliver creative effectiveness instead of creative excellence. He calls the idea that these are opposites chilling.

A question for you too

Worth arguing about in your group

Burger King has won at Cannes, won at the Clios, and invented tactics the whole industry copied. It is still third by US sales. Does that mean the creative work failed? Or does it mean creative work was never going to fix a problem that lives in restaurant counts and ad budgets? Your answer decides what kind of campaign you build.

07 · The delivery

One question this week. Only the analysis.

Last time the benchmark was half the grade and the campaign was the other half, which meant the benchmark got done fast so the fun part could start. Not this time. This week the benchmark is the whole thing.

Say where the opportunity sits, and why
1
Benchmark McDonald's against Burger King 100% of the grade
Use the Benchmark tool. Compare the two brands globally and in Colombia, for each of the three age segments. Your analysis has to let you:
  • Compare competitiveness in each of the six grids, judging both brands on the same attributes.
  • Name three strengths and three weaknesses per brand, per segment. Six judgements per grid, thirty-six per brand in total.
  • Show where global and Colombia disagree, if they do. That gap is usually the most interesting thing in the whole delivery.
  • Identify one segment with a real opportunity in it, name which brand it favours, and explain why that segment and not the other five.
Where you will actually use this. Benchmarking is not a school exercise that stops existing after the course. It is how a company decides which market to enter, which product to launch first, what to fix before a relaunch and what it can get away with charging. It is also the fastest way to find a hole: a place where every brand in the category is weak, or where something people would happily buy is simply not being sold. Businesses get built on holes that somebody bothered to measure first. The grid you are filling in this week is the same grid a real team fills in before spending real money. Theirs just has more rows.

The one thing that separates a 5 from a 3

Anybody can list six things about a brand. What is being graded is whether the same attributes were applied to both columns, whether the judgements change from segment to segment (if 18–24 and 35–44 came out identical, the segmentation did not happen), and whether the Colombian grids show you actually went and looked.

08 · How you are graded

The rubric, in full

The scale is the same five-point scale from the Nike and Adidas delivery. What changed is that it now covers the whole grade instead of half of it, so there is no campaign to make up for a thin analysis.

100%
Benchmark analysis
The six grids, three strengths and three weaknesses per brand per segment, the global and Colombian comparison, and the opportunity you identified with the reason behind it.
5
Excellent use of benchmark. All six grids complete, the same attributes applied to both brands, segments clearly different from each other, Colombia researched rather than assumed, opportunity identified and well argued.
4
Good use of benchmark. Clear comparison and a defensible opportunity, but some grids thinner than others or the local research lighter than the global.
3
Adequate use of benchmark and a reasonable opportunity, but the segmentation adds little: the age groups or the two geographies read almost the same.
2
Basic or weak use of benchmark. Attributes not matched between brands, grids incomplete, or the opportunity asserted without support.
1
Little or no effective use of benchmark. Two descriptions side by side instead of a comparison.

What is being looked at, in order of weight

Are the attributes matched?

The same list judged on both sides. This is the difference between a benchmark and two profiles, and it is the first thing that gets checked.

Are the six grids actually different from each other?

If you can swap the 18–24 grid with the 35–44 grid and nobody notices, the segmentation was decorative.

Is Colombia researched or assumed?

The global grids can come from reading. The local ones cannot, and it shows immediately which teams went and looked.

Does the opportunity follow from the grids?

It has to be traceable to something you wrote down, not to a hunch you had before you started.

Why this one is worth the effort

Benchmarking is the least glamorous thing in this course and the one that decides everything downstream. A campaign is only ever as good as the reading of the market underneath it, and this is where you learn to do that reading. Get good at the boring half and the interesting half stops being guesswork.
