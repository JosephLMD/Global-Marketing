01 · Start here

Start here

Nike and Adidas sell very similar things: shoes, shirts, tracksuits. If products were all that mattered, the two companies would be roughly the same size. They are not. That gap was built with marketing, and this case is about how.

$310bn
Size of the global sporting goods market
~60 years
How long the rivalry has run
1924
Year Adidas's story starts, in Germany
1964
Year Nike's story starts, in Oregon

Words you will need

Brand love
When people feel something for a brand, not just buy it. Hard to measure, very hard to copy.
Positioning
The place a brand tries to occupy in your head. "The hero." "The cool lifestyle one."
Endorsement
Paying an athlete or celebrity to wear and represent your brand.
Collaboration (collab)
A limited product made with an outside designer, musician or brand, to borrow their audience.
Purpose marketing
Building the brand around a value or a cause, not around the product.
Benchmark
Putting two companies side by side on the same criteria, so the comparison is fair.

What you have to do

Read the case

Sections 02 to 05. It is a story, so read it like one. You can open the original article too, but everything you need is here.

Build the Benchmark

Put Nike and Adidas side by side, globally and locally. Use it to find one target audience or market where Adidas has an opportunity, and say why. That is Question 1, worth 50%.

Create the Adidas campaign

From that same opportunity: a campaign name, a brief with five parts, and at least two pieces of promotional material. That is Question 2, the other 50%.

02 · The story

The story, in order

Blue dots are Adidas. Black dots are Nike. Notice how often the important moment is an advert or a partnership, not a new shoe.

1924 · Germany
Two brothers start a shoe factory
Adi and Rudolf Dassler make some of the first spiked running shoes. Olympic runners Lina Radke and Jesse Owens wear them. Adi's method: meet runners, listen, improve the shoe.
After a family fight
The brothers split: Adidas and Puma are born
Adi keeps going under his own name. Rudolf starts what becomes Puma. Adidas leads running shoes for nearly two decades.
Early 1960s · Oregon
A student writes a paper
Phil Knight, at Stanford business school, argues that cheap, good Japanese running shoes could take the US market from the Germans, the way Japanese cameras already had. He goes home and starts Blue Ribbon Sports with his old track coach, Bill Bowerman. Phil Knight interviewed in Harvard Business Review
Mid-1970s
Blue Ribbon Sports becomes Nike
New name, the swoosh logo, and the first athlete deal: tennis player Ilie Năstase. Sales are doubling every year. Nike had spotted a gap nobody was serving: there was barely a market for shoes made specifically for running. Knight on how Nike grew
1976 · The turning point
"There Is No Finish Line"
Nike's first real ad. It barely talks about the shoe. It talks about how running feels. This is the moment Nike stops selling footwear and starts selling an idea, and the whole modern playbook follows from it. See the original poster
1986
Run-D.M.C. record "My Adidas"
The Superstar basketball shoe jumps from the court to the street because a hip-hop group wears it. Adidas learns something Nike had not: you do not only need athletes. Culture sells shoes too. Listen to "My Adidas" And "Walk This Way", the crossover that made them famous
1988
"Just Do It"
Later ranked one of the best taglines of the entire 20th century. With Michael Jordan's Air line behind it, Nike's revenue passes $1.2 billion. Ad Age ranks the top slogans of the century
1992
Adidas almost dies
Record losses, close to bankruptcy. New boss Robert Louis-Dreyfus turns it from a company that sells things into a company that markets things. The 1995 slogan says it out loud: "We knew then. We know now." Adidas through the 1990s
1990s
Nike buys attention, and edge
With agency Wieden + Kennedy: "I am Not a Role Model" with Charles Barkley, and "If You Let Me Play" about girls in sport. Nike is now a brand with opinions. Watch "If You Let Me Play"
2000 · The scoreboard
Nike ≈ $9bn. Adidas ≈ $5bn.
Both then buy their way into new categories. Nike takes Cole Haan, Bauer Hockey and Converse (2003). Adidas takes Salomon (1997) and then Reebok for $3.8 billion (2006). What happened to Reebok after the deal
2018
"Dream Crazy" with Colin Kaepernick
"Believe in something. Even if it means sacrificing everything." Huge controversy, and Nike's brand value rises $6 billion. The lesson people took: a risky stance can pay, if the brand has been consistent about its values. What happened when the Kaepernick ad ran
2000 onwards
Adidas plays the long sustainability game
It starts publishing an annual social and environmental report from 2000, years before most brands. That early move is worth a lot later, when younger buyers start caring. The whole reporting archive, from 2000 on
Late 2021
Both jump into the metaverse
Nike builds "Nikeland" on Roblox and buys RTFKT, a virtual sneaker studio. Adidas builds in The Sandbox and does an NFT with Bored Ape Yacht Club. Same instinct, different bets. Marketing Dive on Nikeland
2024
"Winning Isn't for Everyone"
A gritty Olympics campaign narrated by Willem Dafoe, with LeBron James and Serena Williams, about the ruthless streak that separates winners. It was meant to restart Nike after a slump that ended with the chief executive leaving. Marketing Dive on the campaign
2025
"So Win", and the Super Bowl return
Nike went back to the Super Bowl after nearly thirty years away, with a spot about women in sport starring Jordan Chiles, Caitlin Clark, Sha'Carri Richardson and the rapper Doechii. It won a Super Clio. Then Nike skipped the following year's game, exactly while women's sport kept growing. Marketing Dive on the Super Bowl spot
2023 to 2026
Adidas and Bad Bunny
A long partnership with one of the biggest artists in the world, and a clear example of Adidas doing what it has always done: reaching people through culture rather than through sport. It arrives in Colombia too, which is worth thinking about when you pick your target. Adidas Colombia, Bad Bunny
April 2026
The Boston Marathon misfire
An outdoor ad read "Runners Welcome. Walkers Tolerated." Disability advocates objected, rivals pounced, and Nike pulled it. The brand's own mission says "if you have a body, you are an athlete", so the line contradicted the brand. A useful reminder that tone is part of positioning. Marketing Dive on where the comeback is stumbling
2026 · World Cup
Both brands go all in
Adidas released "Backyard Legends" with Timothée Chalamet, Lamine Yamal, Jude Bellingham and Trinity Rodman. Nike answered with "Rip the Script". Nike won the views by roughly ten to one. Adidas won the attribution: people remembered who the sponsor was. Marketing Dive compares the two campaigns

03 · How they think

Two brands, two completely different bets

This is the heart of the case. Both sell sportswear. Neither is trying to occupy the same space in your head.

"Both brands deliver a feeling, a specific value that goes beyond the functional benefits of wearing the shoes."

Niels Neudecker, head of brand performance, Kantar
Nike
The hero
  • Wants to be the hero. To challenge the status quo, which is exactly how an athlete thinks.
  • Sells the feeling, not the shoe. That started in 1976 and never stopped.
  • Takes public stances. Kaepernick is the clearest example, and it worked.
  • Spends big on advertising. Its budget is larger, and it uses it on ads.
  • Positions as sportswear: performance first.
Adidas
The lifestyle
  • Not the hero, the lifestyle. Its own mission statement uses that word.
  • Lives in culture. Musicians, artists and designers, going back to Run-D.M.C. in 1986.
  • Moved early on sustainability: reporting since 2001, long before it was expected.
  • Spends on sponsorship rather than ads: teams, tournaments, individual players.
  • Positions as streetwear and lifestyle: cool first.
Worth noticing. Neither position is "better". They are different answers to the same question: why should someone love us? When you build your campaign in Question 2, you are choosing an answer to that same question, so it has to fit the brand Adidas already is.

"You have to reinvent yourself but be consistent with your purpose. It's important that you know what you stand for and innovate within this space."

Niels Neudecker, Kantar

04 · The battlegrounds

Five places where they actually fight

They almost never attack each other directly, with no name-calling, unlike burgers or colas. The fight happens in these five arenas instead.

Athletes and celebrities

Adidas invented this game: Adi Dassler was fitting Olympians in the 1920s. Nike industrialised it with Jordan. Today Adidas leans on musicians and designers, Nike on athletes.

Started by Adidas · scaled by Nike

Football

Adidas has supplied the official FIFA World Cup match ball since 1970, and sponsors national teams, leagues and clubs. Nike keeps chipping away: an app in 2014, and the US women's league sponsored through 2030.

Adidas's fortress

Collaborations

Limited drops that borrow someone else's audience. Adidas: Beyoncé, Pharrell Williams, Stella McCartney, Prada, Gucci. Nike: Travis Scott, Supreme, Drake. Both are described as "pretty vicious" here.

Genuinely even

Values and purpose

Nike goes loud on social issues. Adidas goes quiet and long on the environment. Two different ways of saying "we stand for something".

Different weapons

Digital and the metaverse

Nike: Nikeland on Roblox, a Snapchat AR lens, and buying RTFKT for virtual sneakers. Adidas: The Sandbox and a Bored Ape NFT. Both moved in the same month of 2021.

Too early to call
A moment worth remembering. At the 2014 World Cup, Adidas sponsored the tournament and both teams in the final. Nike was not even the sponsor, and still stole the attention with a five-minute animated ad, "The Last Game", which became one of the most shared posts on Facebook. Owning the rights is not the same as winning the moment.

05 · What comes next

Where this is heading

Both companies are enormous. That is starting to be a problem, not only an advantage.

"While they continue to show up in all the big ways on all the big platforms, they'll need to engage at deeper levels to achieve the intimacy, nuance and localization that are native to the scrappier upstarts."

Jack Bedwani, CEO, New Moon

Three pressures

Big is no longer automatically good. The challenge is not to do more or go bigger. It is to show up in a way that feels local and personal, which is the one thing huge global brands find hardest.

Smaller brands are taking the culture. New Balance and FILA are working with tastemakers in ways that feel fresher, and Gen Z gravitates to resellers and cultural figures rather than to giants.

Being loud stopped being enough. Even Nike has not repeated the impact of "Dream Crazy". What keeps a brand relevant now is credibility with a specific culture, not volume.

"You can't remain on top unless you're fighting to stay there. And part of that is keeping your name out there."

Nicholas Smith, author of "Kicks: The Great American Story of Sneakers"

06 · The delivery

What you have to deliver

Objective. Analyze Nike and Adidas using the Benchmark tool, identify an opportunity for Adidas, and turn that opportunity into a marketing campaign.

Benchmark
↓
Identify opportunity
↓
Select target
↓
Create Adidas campaign
↓
Promotional material

Each step feeds the next. That chain is the assignment.

1
Benchmark analysis 50% of the grade
Using the Benchmark tool, compare Nike vs. Adidas at a global and local level. Your analysis has to let you:
  • Compare the competitiveness of both brands
  • Identify the main differences between Nike and Adidas
  • Identify one target audience or market where Adidas has an opportunity
  • Explain why that target represents an opportunity for Adidas
2
Create the Adidas campaign 50% of the grade
Using the target and the opportunity you identified in Question 1, build a campaign for Adidas. It has to show how Adidas can take advantage of that opportunity.
Campaign name 10%

A creative, memorable name that connects with your target and the opportunity.

Campaign brief 20%

Five things, briefly:

  • Target: who are you talking to?
  • Opportunity: what did you identify?
  • Objective: what should the campaign achieve?
  • Big idea: what is the central idea?
  • Key message: what should they remember?
Promotional material 20%

At least two pieces. For example: a social media post, a billboard, a digital ad, a video concept, an influencer activation, an event, an experiential activation or an OOH campaign.

The question your whole grade turns on
Did you use the opportunity identified through the Benchmark to create a relevant campaign for Adidas?
This link is worth 50% of Question 2 on its own, more than the name, the brief and the materials put together. A beautiful campaign that does not come out of your Benchmark loses half of Question 2 before anything else is judged.
About using AI. Use it to help gather your Benchmark: it is fast at pulling numbers together. But it is confidently wrong quite often, and it will give you a 2023 figure when you asked for this year. So: use it, then check it. Every number needs a source and a date. If you could not verify something, keep it and write unverified next to it. That is honest work, not weak work. Add one line saying which tool you used and what you checked.
